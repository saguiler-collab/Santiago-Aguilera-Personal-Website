# Outside the Walls

## Where this happened

In the summer of 2026, I spent eleven days shadowing at the **Torre Pediátrica of
the Hospital de Alta Especialidad de Veracruz (HAEV)** — a public, second-level
hospital operating under Mexico's IMSS-Bienestar system, which serves families
with no other access to healthcare. I split my time between pediatric surgery
consultations and operating rooms, a pediatric allergy and immunology
consultory, and morning rounds with the surgical team.

I was there to learn medicine. I left with a different kind of education
entirely.

## What I saw

Over nine days I watched an appendectomy on a four-year-old, a laparoscopic
gallbladder removal on a six-year-old, a circumcision on a nine-year-old who
had lived with untreated pain for years simply because no one had told his
family it could be fixed, and the staged reduction of a newborn's omphalocele —
improvised with colloid dressings and elastic bands because the equipment the
procedure typically calls for wasn't available in the building.

In the consultation rooms, I sat in on cases spanning genetics, neonatology,
and allergy and immunology. One case in particular stayed with me: a young
girl with facial asymmetry and distinctive skin findings, whose presentation —
cross-referenced against her mother's nearly identical features — pointed to a
genetic condition affecting fewer than 300 confirmed cases worldwide,
undiagnosed in the family for a lifetime.

I also watched what doesn't make it into a clinical case study: children
arriving from five hours away because this was the nearest hospital equipped
to treat them; families who had lost their own medical records between
visits; a surgeon explaining, patiently, for the third time that visit, when
a family should return — because there was no piece of paper to remind them.

## What the hospital is working against

None of what I'm about to describe reflects a lack of skill or effort from the
people working there. If anything, the opposite. What I saw was a level of
clinical improvisation and care that exceeded what I expected walking in. What
I'm describing is the system around them.

**No reliable internet in the building.** Some patient documentation is still
produced on typewriters, because there aren't enough computers to go around.

**Physicians personally fund basic equipment.** Printers, laptops, even
protective gear are, in some cases, purchased out of pocket by the doctors who
need them — not supplied by the institution.

**A language and literacy gap with no formal bridge.** A large share of the
families arriving at this hospital cannot read, or do not speak Spanish as a
first language. Some travel in from mountain communities where neighboring
villages speak mutually unintelligible languages. The hospital has no
interpreters on staff.

**Prescriptions that don't go home with the family.** By design, the pharmacy
retains the physical prescription once medication is dispensed — which means a
family can walk out with no written record of what their own child is taking,
for how long, or why.

**A common diagnosis that resists easy detection.** Appendicitis is the most
frequent pediatric abdominal surgical emergency, but in published pediatric
case series, roughly half of children do not present with the textbook signs —
no migrating pain, no rebound tenderness. Delay in recognition and treatment,
not the surgery itself, is what turns a treatable case into a fatal one.

Individually, each of these is a known problem somewhere in global health.
Together, in one building, they compound: a family that can't read the
instructions they were given verbally, holding no paperwork to reference later,
managing a condition that may not look the way they were told to expect it to
look.

## What I built because of it

That gap — between what a physician explains in a rushed consultation and what
a family actually carries home and understands — is what I started building a
tool to close.

**SanaSanita** is a free, offline, picture-first care-plan tool designed for
IMSS-Bienestar pediatric hospitals. A physician fills out a short form; the
tool generates a one-page plan — printed or on a phone — that shows, in
drawings a non-reading parent can follow, what happened, what to feed the
child, when to give medication, what warning signs mean it's time to come back
immediately, and when the next appointment is. It requires no internet to use
in the hospital, stores no patient-identifying data of any kind, and no
content reaches a family until a physician has reviewed and signed off on it
line by line.

It is still in development. It has not been formally adopted by any hospital,
and I'm not overstating that: what I have so far is a working prototype, real
field research, and productive early conversations with the physicians I
shadowed under. The next step isn't more features — it's finding the right
institutional partner to make the clinical review process legitimate and to
carry this beyond one hospital.

## Why this mattered to me

I've spent the past year at a boarding school in the United States, with
access to laboratories, mentorship, and academic opportunity that most
students in this hemisphere will never see. Standing in that hospital, the
distance between what I have access to and what those physicians have access
to wasn't a distance in skill, effort, or intelligence — I watched people do
more with less than I thought was possible. It was a distance in tools,
infrastructure, and access.

That distance is not permanent, and it's not someone else's problem to close.
It's the direction I want to spend my work pointed at: building the research,
the tools, and eventually the organizations that bring the kind of medical and
educational opportunity I've had access to back into the region my family
comes from.
