CSR-x

A systematic review of 33 studies on PAH exposure, epigenetic aging and lung cancer risk.

PAHs, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, are wherever something has been burned: exhaust, cigarette smoke, charred food. At CSR-x, with a small remote cohort, I spent five weeks reviewing the evidence on what chronic exposure to them does at the level of the genome, not just the lungs, and we wrote it up as a paper and a poster.

Five weeks, remote, student-led

CSR-x, the Collaborative Summer Research eXperience, is a selective remote program with no professor running the room. I was in the Biomedical and Health Sciences track, Cohort #2: five mentees and two mentors building one piece of research together.

  • TracksAI and data science, biomedical and health sciences, environmental science, cognitive science, and economics. Mine was biomedical.
  • WhenJuly to August 2026. The last database searches were run between July 28 and August 3.

The question

PAHs, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, are organic compounds made of two or more fused aromatic rings, formed when organic material burns incompletely. People meet them at work in coke-oven work, coal mining and chimney sweeping, at home through smoky coal, and every day through vehicle emissions, tobacco smoke and charred meat.

Our research question: how is PAH exposure associated with epigenetic age acceleration and lung cancer risk?

  • Epigenetic ageAn estimate of biological aging from DNA methylation patterns, compared with how old someone actually is.
  • The gapFew studies had looked at the full pathway from exposure to epigenetic aging to lung cancer, and none had measured all of it in one population.

How the review was done

The review follows the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews, from a record-level screening tracker through to an evidence matrix, an evidence map and a heterogeneity analysis.

  • SearchPubMed, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar, with 22 search terms combined with AND and OR, including “PAH exposure”, “DNA methylation”, “epigenetic age acceleration” and “benzo[a]pyrene”.
  • ScreenOne of five reviewers screened each record: duplicates, title and abstract, full text, then an evidence pathway and an evidence category (primary, mechanistic or background).
  • ExtractEach included study went into a standardised evidence matrix: design, population, sample size, exposure biomarker, methylation method, epigenetic clock, effect estimate, confidence interval, confounders, strengths and limitations.
  • SynthesiseFive evidence pathways, each rated strong, moderate, limited, inconsistent, mechanistically plausible or not adequately studied, followed by an analysis of heterogeneity.
04

From 53 records to 33 studies

The PRISMA flow chart in the paper tracks every record.

  • Identified53 records: PubMed 16, Google Scholar 29, ScienceDirect 8.
  • Screened3 duplicates removed, leaving 50; 9 excluded.
  • Retrieved41 sought, 6 not retrieved, 35 assessed for eligibility.
  • Included2 excluded (one did not meet the inclusion criteria, one had an ineligible design), leaving 33 studies.
05

The 33 studies

The evidence base is varied, which is both its strength and the reason the studies are hard to compare.

  • Human or mechanisticNearly 73% of the studies were in humans; the rest were cell and animal studies of mechanism.
  • DesignsCross-sectional 14, case-control 8, longitudinal 2, plus repeated-measures, in vitro and Mendelian randomization designs.
  • Where14 of the 33 studies were done in China, and occupational cohorts were overrepresented overall.
  • SamplesUrine for internal PAH exposure, blood for DNA methylation, and buccal and lung cells.
  • ClocksFirst-generation (Horvath, Hannum, Zhang) and second-generation (PhenoAge, GrimAge).
06

What we found: exposure and DNA methylation

PAH exposure was consistently associated with altered nuclear and mitochondrial DNA methylation, most often as reduced methylation. The same loci kept appearing: AHRR, F2RL3, ACBD6, CYP1A1, GSTP1 and GSTM1 in the nucleus, and MT-ATP6 and MT-ATP8 in the mitochondria.

Smoking complicates it: tobacco smoke is a major source of PAHs and also changes methylation at AHRR and F2RL3 on its own.

07

What we found: exposure and epigenetic aging

The link to faster epigenetic aging was moderate to strong, and clearest with second-generation clocks such as GrimAge and PhenoAge.

  • Coke-oven workersIn Poland, 40.8% of exposed workers had a DNA methylation age above their real age, against 15.6% of controls (p = 0.007).
  • Household coalIn never-smoking women in Xuanwei, China, each standard-deviation rise in household PAH exposure went with 0.77 more years of GrimAge acceleration (95% CI 0.36 to 1.19).
  • Timing mattersIn young children, current exposure went with older epigenetic age, while prenatal estimates pointed the other way but were not significant.
  • The clock mattersIn 73 healthy adults, only PhenoAge acceleration was significantly linked to the chemical mixture.
08

What we found: epigenetic aging and lung cancer

Faster epigenetic aging predicted later lung cancer in observational studies, but a Mendelian randomization study did not support a causal effect.

  • Never-smokersEach extra year of GrimAge acceleration went with 19% higher odds of lung cancer (95% CI 1.06 to 1.34), in 80 cases and 83 controls in Shanghai.
  • Over 17 years146 incident lung cancers, with a dose-response across PhenoAge, GrimAge and a mortality risk score.
  • The cautionEpigenetic aging looks like a marker of risk, not a proven cause.
09

What we found: exposure and lung cancer

This was the best-supported link in the whole pathway.

  • OccupationalThe pooled SYNERGY study: 16,901 cases and 20,965 controls across 14 case-control studies in Europe and Canada. Cumulative benzo[a]pyrene exposure was linked to lung cancer after adjusting for smoking, with odds ratios of 1.11 in men and 1.21 in women.
  • HouseholdIn 1,015 never-smoking women in Xuanwei and Fuyuan, each standard-deviation rise in a cluster of 25 PAHs went with an odds ratio of 2.21.
  • Internal doseEach standard-deviation rise in blood BPDE-albumin adducts, a marker of activated benzo[a]pyrene, went with an odds ratio of 1.46 (p = 0.005).
  • MethylationFive CpG sites statistically accounted for about 58% of the link between benzo[a]pyrene and lung cancer, which fits a methylation pathway but is not proof of one.
10

What we found: the mechanism

The mechanistic studies fit together: the body turns benzo[a]pyrene into a reactive metabolite, BPDE, which can bind DNA and trigger oxidative stress and DNA damage.

  • Oxidative stressA 50% rise in urinary 1-hydroxypyrene went with an 11 to 13% rise in oxidative stress biomarkers.
  • DNA damageFirefighters exposed to wildfire smoke showed 22% more DNA damage on a comet assay, although smoke is a mixture.
  • In cellsHuman lung cells exposed to benzo[a]pyrene formed DNA adducts and micronuclei, and their cell cycle was disrupted.
11

How certain is it?

Certainty decreases as the pathway goes downstream. The whole pathway is biologically plausible but still inferred, because no study measured every step in one group of people.

  • StrongPAH exposure and lung cancer, in occupational, household and internal-dose studies.
  • Highly supportive, but variedPAH exposure with DNA methylation and with epigenetic age acceleration.
  • Least establishedEpigenetic aging as the thing that actually carries PAH exposure to lung cancer.
12

The limits, and what comes next

The paper is explicit about what it cannot say.

  • DesignsMost studies were cross-sectional or case-control, so they cannot show cause and effect over time.
  • ExposureMany relied on urinary metabolites, which reflect recent exposure, not the chronic exposure that lung cancer takes years to follow.
  • MixturesPAHs came mixed with tobacco smoke, coal dust and particulates, so single compounds were hard to isolate.
  • GeneralizabilityOccupational cohorts and studies from China were overrepresented, and blood stood in for lung tissue.
  • NextA prospective cohort that measures exposure, several epigenetic clocks and lung cancer outcomes in the same people, with repeated exposure measures, paired blood and lung samples and more varied populations.
