## What this site is

The site is called the Santiago Aguilera Library. The idea running through it is a personal library of everything six countries left behind. A student, a researcher, an athlete, and a musician, collected and kept in order.

Most of the shape is already built. What is missing is real text in several spots. A number of pages currently show a box that says something like "Still to add" or "Waiting on details." The job here is to turn those into real sentences, in Santiago's own words.

## The one rule that matters most

Never guess. Never fill a blank with something that sounds good but is not confirmed. If you do not have the fact, ask for it. If Santiago gives a short or messy answer, tighten the grammar a little. Do not dress it up.

## How to sound like Santiago and not like an AI

* Keep sentences short. If a sentence needs two commas and an "and," split it into two sentences.
* Do not use a dash to join two thoughts. Use a period instead, or "and."
* Skip big or fancy words when a plain one works. "Used" instead of "utilized." "Found" instead of "identified."
* Cut phrases like "passionate about," "leverage," "cutting edge," "in today's world," "at the intersection of," "seamless," "robust." None of these belong on this site.
* It is fine for a line to sound plain or a little unfinished. That is what makes it sound like a real person, not a brand.
* Before saving any finished line, read it out loud. If it sounds like a college essay or a pitch deck, cut it down.

## The process for every section

1. Find a spot marked "Still to add," "Waiting on details," or anything else clearly unfinished.
2. Ask Santiago one or two short, direct questions about it. Not five at once.
3. Once he answers, write two or three short versions of the line. Make them genuinely different in angle or length, not just the same sentence reworded.
4. Show him the options. Let him pick one, combine them, or write his own version instead.
5. Only then put the final text into the file.
6. Move to the next section.

Do not skip steps 2 through 4, even if the answer feels obvious.

## Background for asking better questions

This section is not text to copy onto the site. It exists so your questions are specific instead of generic. Confirm every detail with Santiago before using it, since some of this may need updating.

**CellAtlas GBM.** A team project on glioblastoma, the most aggressive kind of brain tumor. The team used public data from TCGA (a large cancer genomics database), specifically RNA sequencing data from GBM tumors. They used a few methods (NNLS, SVR, and BayesPrism) to estimate what cell types make up each tumor, and built a survival model (a Cox model with a ridge penalty) linking cell makeup to patient survival. Santiago's part was building the reference benchmark and doing independent checks on the team's numbers, plus building scoring tools and a rule that kept the team limited to seven confirmed cell types so nothing got added without data behind it. The project was a finalist at a competition and has its own live site, built with Next.js, React, Three.js, and Tailwind, deployed on Vercel. Good questions: which competition and what happened there, what he wants people to notice first, whether he wants the live link front and center.

**SanaSanita, the hospital app.** Built using what Santiago learned during a summer program at Carnegie Mellon, aimed at helping the pediatric hospital in Veracruz where he was volunteering. He built it and tried to present it to hospital leadership. He was told the hospital could not act on something informal like that without backing from a university and his own school. He was given a contact to follow up with once that backing is in place. Good questions: does he want the page to say where things stand now, or just describe the app itself and why he built it. What does the app actually do, screen by screen. What does he want a stranger to understand about it in the first ten seconds.

**The PAH and epigenetic aging review.** Done through CSR-x, a summer research program, with four other students and a mentor. The topic is whether exposure to certain chemicals called PAHs (found in things like smoke and exhaust) speeds up aging at the cell level, and whether that raises the risk of lung cancer. Santiago built a large table tracking 33 studies and did a close read of seven of them. The most interesting finding is that no single study has tested all three parts of that chain, the exposure, the aging, and the cancer risk, in the same group of people. Some research groups have studied separate pieces of the chain without connecting them yet. Good questions: does he want to frame this as ongoing or as a finished piece of work. Does he want to mention the poster.

**Hospital shadowing in Veracruz.** Santiago shadowed at a pediatric hospital for more than sixty hours, mostly with a pediatric surgeon. He kept long notes almost every day, sometimes ten pages, on the cases and procedures he saw. Partway through, the contact who first brought him in left, and he kept going by asking the head of pediatric surgery directly. Good question: is there one specific day or case he wants to describe. One real moment reads better than a summary of the hours.

**School leadership.** Santiago started the HOSA chapter at Perkiomen, where none existed before him. He co-edits the school paper, the Perkiomenite, and runs the newsroom at a Model UN conference. He is part of Model UN, including something called GVMUN, and was part of student council and an investment club. Good questions: for each one, what did he actually do day to day, and is there a specific result or moment that shows why it mattered. A title alone does not say much.

**Sports and music.** Santiago swims varsity, does Olympic lifting, and plays drums, having played in three bands since starting at a music school called SonoArte in Panama. Good questions: does he want any times or numbers from swimming, how long he has trained, whether he wants the bands named.
